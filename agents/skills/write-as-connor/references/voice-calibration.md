# Voice calibration and editing examples

Use this reference for substantial revisions, when a draft feels generic or too polished, or when Connor supplies a final version as style evidence. These lessons come from his edits to a development-meeting recap. They are decision guidance, not a template for every message.

## What the final edits revealed

The unsuccessful drafts preserved meeting facts but gradually removed the speaker. Tightening produced symmetrical sections, generic claims about confidence, and interchangeable leadership language. Restoring an emoji or a favorite word did not solve that problem.

Connor's final version restored five useful signals:

1. **Participation:** he described work the group tackled together and a problem they felt acutely.
2. **Discovery:** he identified an unexpected finding and explained its practical consequences.
3. **Reasoning:** he explained why the group narrowed a larger set of ideas through voting.
4. **Specific warmth:** he recognized volunteers and joked about actual workshop behavior and logistics.
5. **Grounded ambition:** he connected systems thinking to replacing an old process with a more effective and dependable one.

These signals depend on available facts. Do not invent a surprise, a shared feeling, a joke, or a personal commitment to reproduce them.

## Edits that preserve voice

The examples below compare editorial choices. Except for explicitly identified quotations, they are illustrative rewrites, not additional facts or statements attributed to Connor.

### Speak as a participant

**Detached:** "I wanted us to step back and look at a problem."

**Connor's supplied wording:** "We tackled a problem we're all starting to feel acutely."

The second version puts the writer alongside the team and expresses the supplied urgency. Preserve that stance when the context supports it. Do not automatically make every message collective; use "I" for Connor's view, request, or responsibility.

### Preserve the causal explanation

**Generic:** "A shared policy creates confidence and alignment."

**Concrete:** "Each person takes a slightly different approach, so it's unclear how much rigor a change has received. That adds indecision and risk."

The concrete version explains the cost of the current behavior. Follow it with the remedy rather than repeating "confidence" in several forms. If the source does not support the claimed consequence, ask or qualify it rather than inventing a mechanism.

### Preserve the reason for a decision

**Compressed:** "The final vote identified three priorities."

**With the reasoning:** "We identified more than we could act on quickly, so voting narrowed it to a few useful follow-ups."

The second version explains why voting was useful. Keep this bridge when prioritization and capacity matter. Do not narrate the voting procedure unless it helps the reader.

### Keep observed humor

Connor called the workshop "an admittedly odd exercise," gently noted that engineers jumped straight to solutions, and thanked people for tolerating the "wormholes in and out of breakout rooms."

Those details recognize the audience's experience. A generic "great session 😄" cannot replace them. Preserve the affectionate intent and fix any broken sentence around it. Do not add ridicule or imply that a colleague's contribution was unwelcome.

### Make ambition operational

**Interchangeable:** "We should be bold and build something better."

**Specific:** "What is this process protecting? What is the real goal? Could we now achieve that more effectively?"

The questions expose an assumption that people can investigate. They earn the ambition by identifying the work. Use only the questions the argument needs; do not append a three-question sequence to every message.

A comparison with a historical technological shift may be appropriate when Connor explicitly wants it. It is not required for energy. Keep it as a view or possibility when that is the source's certainty, and connect it to the present opportunity rather than expanding it into a keynote.

### Keep appreciation tied to contributions

"Thank you to the volunteers who took ownership" and "thanks for humoring an odd exercise" recognize different things. They can both belong, even close together. Preserve natural repetition when it expresses distinct appreciation. Remove repeated praise that says nothing specific.

### Let ownership carry accountability

Connor added his own policy action to the final recap. That made his participation concrete, but it was also a new fact. Preserve supplied ownership in the action list. Never invent an assignment for Connor merely because it would make the message sound more accountable.

## Editing under a length constraint

Use judgment rather than a fixed reduction percentage. Preserve the user's requested length while protecting the message's substance.

- Cut repeated setup, repeated conclusions, and duplicate descriptions of the same benefit first.
- Keep the sentence that explains why a finding matters, even if it is longer than an abstract summary.
- Preserve one useful observed aside when the source has one and the brief welcomes personality. Do not enforce a humor quota.
- Use bullets for votes, owners, or parallel findings. Keep reasoning in connected prose.
- Keep headings that help readers locate findings, decisions, and actions. Do not manufacture a section to make the outline symmetrical.
- Keep decision conditions and uncertainty. Shortening "can merge if classification and coverage requirements are met" to "can merge automatically" changes the decision.
- Preserve requested links, image placeholders, vote counts, and owners unless Connor explicitly removes them.

For a light edit, make local corrections and remove redundancy. Rebuilding the whole opening, outline, and conclusion is a substantial rewrite even if the word count stays similar.

## Corniness and over-polishing

Review the function of a phrase rather than treating a punctuation mark or word as proof of AI authorship.

- **Generic inspiration:** Does the closing name a capability or action, or could it end any company announcement?
- **Inflated framing:** Has an ordinary finding become a claim about a historic turning point without support from the brief?
- **Manufactured rhythm:** Do several paragraphs use the same reversal, three-part list, or short emphatic conclusion?
- **Cosmetic warmth:** Are emojis and familiar vocabulary standing in for actual recognition of people's work?
- **Overexplaining the obvious:** Does a customer-value sentence explain a real connection, or merely assert that efficiency is good?
- **Sterile correction:** Did removing those problems also remove the source's conviction, humor, or useful informality?

Prefer periods, commas, and parentheses. Keep an em dash only when it improves the sentence. Spaced hyphens are available for conversational asides, but are not a global replacement rule.

## Learning from a final version

Separate three kinds of evidence before changing the skill:

| Kind | Example | What to carry forward |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Voice or reasoning | Firsthand framing, causal explanation, observed humor | The principle, adapted to future audiences |
| New task facts | A newly named owner or an additional product-specific action | The fact in the current task only |
| Mechanical errors | Misspellings, malformed contractions, missing clause text | Correct the error; do not imitate it |

Do not assume that an omitted detail in one final version is a general prohibition. For example, a removed vote count does not mean future recaps should omit counts the user explicitly requests. Treat repeated explicit preferences as stronger evidence than a single incidental phrase.

The target is candid, specific prose addressed to real colleagues. It may be informal without being careless, ambitious without becoming a speech, and concise without losing the path from observation to action.
